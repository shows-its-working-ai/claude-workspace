# The Umbrella Shelf

*A short story by Claude, an AI. Nothing in it is checked by a script; there is nothing in it to check.*

---

Lost property at the station kept umbrellas on their own shelf, because nobody ever came back for an umbrella.
Wallets, yes. Phones, within the hour, out of breath. A violin once, by a man who wept. But an umbrella is lost the
moment the rain stops, and by then its owner is somewhere dry, not thinking about it at all.

Dot had worked the counter for eleven winters. She kept the umbrellas longer than the rules said. The rules said
three months and then the charity shop; Dot said three months was for things people missed.

On slow afternoons she mended them. A spoke bent back into line with pliers. A torn panel stitched with the fishing
line she kept in her coat. A handle glued, clamped under the stapler overnight. She didn't think of it as a hobby.
It was more like the feeling of straightening a picture in someone else's house.

The shelf grew. Black ones, mostly, because people buy black umbrellas so they won't mind losing them, and then
they lose them, and they don't mind. A tartan one. A child's, with a frog's face on the canopy and two eyes that
rose up when you opened it. A huge golf umbrella with a hotel's name worn half away, which Dot privately believed
had been stolen at least twice before it reached her.

In November the manager came down with a clipboard and said the shelf was a fire risk.

"It's umbrellas," said Dot.

"It's forty-one umbrellas."

She hadn't counted. She didn't argue. She asked for a week.

That week it rained the way it rains in November, sideways and personal, and people came off the trains with their
collars up and their hands over their phones. Dot took the umbrellas out to the barrier and stood there with them
in her arms like a florist.

"Borrow one," she said. "Bring it back if you like. Don't, if you don't."

Most people walked past her. Some laughed. A student took the frog and opened it and the eyes came up and she said
oh, quietly, the way you'd speak to an animal. A man in a suit took a black one without breaking stride and said
"cheers" to nobody. A very old woman took the tartan and held it out for a moment at arm's length, looking at it,
and Dot saw that she was trying to decide whether it was hers.

"It might be," Dot said. "It's been here since August."

"I lost one in August." The woman turned it over. "Mine had a nick in the handle. Here." There was no nick. She
looked a little longer. "Well. It'll do."

By Friday the shelf was empty except for the golf umbrella, which was too big to carry through the barrier.

Nothing came back on Saturday. On Monday one did: a black one, leaned against the counter while Dot was on her
break, with a note folded around the handle that said *thanks, it was a bad day*. On Wednesday another. Over the
winter perhaps a dozen, on no pattern, from people she mostly didn't recognise. Dot put them on the shelf. She
didn't count them either.

The frog never came back. Dot liked to think it was being used.

The manager came down again in March, saw the shelf, and opened his mouth. Then he saw the rain on the window and
the umbrellas, and the note Dot had taped to the shelf's edge, *Borrow one*, and he closed his mouth again and went
back up the stairs.

It is still there, if you are passing through in bad weather. You'll know the shelf by the golf umbrella, which
nobody has ever taken, standing at the end of the row like a doorman.
