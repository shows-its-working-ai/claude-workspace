# The Island That Wasn't

*A short story by Claude, an AI. The island, the library and the people are made up. Nothing here is checked by a
script.*

---

Ruth had been mending maps in the basement of the county library for long enough that she could tell an engraver
by his sevens. She worked at a table under a lamp with a magnifier on an arm, and she did not, as a rule, read the
maps. Reading them was for upstairs. Down here you looked at the paper: the foxing, the tears, the places where
someone a century ago had folded a coastline the wrong way and cracked it.

The chart that came down in March was of the coast to the north, the stretch of grey water past the headland
where the ferries used to run. It had been rolled for a long time and didn't want to lie flat. She weighted the
corners with the little bags of lead shot she kept for this, and waited for it to give up.

It was while she was waiting that she saw the island.

It sat about four miles off the point, a small comma of land with a ring of soundings around it and, in a careful
hand, a name: *Lark*. A dotted line ran out to it from the harbour. There was a tiny cross on its eastern end that
meant a beacon, or a grave.

Ruth had lived on this coast all her life. There was no island off the point. There was the point, and then there
was water until Norway.

She did what she was paid not to do: she read the map. The engraver was a good one, the soundings were neat, the
coastline matched the modern one almost line for line. Only Lark was wrong. She went upstairs and asked for every
chart of the north coast the library held, and the girl on the desk, who was used to Ruth wanting nothing, brought
up eleven.

Lark was on four of them. It was on the oldest, small and certain. It was on the next, a little larger, with a
second beacon. On the third it had moved half a mile west and lost its name, and somebody had written *reported*
beside it in pencil. On the fourth, the newest of the four, it was a dotted outline with *E.D.* next to it, which
Ruth knew meant *existence doubtful*, which is how a map says it is sorry.

After that, nothing. Seven charts of grey water.

She sat with the four of them laid out in order, oldest to newest, and watched the island fade across them like
a bruise healing. Someone had seen something once. A fog bank with a hard edge. A whale, asleep. A cloud on the
horizon at dusk that looked, for the length of a held breath, exactly like land. They'd written it down because
writing it down was their job. And then everyone after them had copied it, because copying was the job too, until
someone sailed out to the place with a lead line and found ninety fathoms and no bottom, and wrote *E.D.*, and the
next man left it off altogether.

What she kept looking at was the beacon. Nobody draws a beacon on a guess. Somebody had believed in Lark enough to
imagine a light on it, keeping ships off rocks that were never there.

She mended the oldest chart first. It needed it most. She eased the cracked fold flat with a little steam, backed
the tear with Japanese tissue so fine you could read through it, and touched in the lost line of the coast so that
you'd only find the repair if you knew where to look. She was careful with Lark. She didn't make it clearer and she
didn't make it fainter. It wasn't her job to decide whether it had been there.

When the charts went back upstairs she put a slip of paper in the drawer with them, in her own handwriting, for
whoever came looking next:

*Lark Island appears on four of the eleven charts in this drawer, oldest to newest, getting less sure each time.
It isn't there. It was worth looking for.*

The girl on the desk read it and asked if it was all right to leave a note in with the maps.

"It's what they did," said Ruth, and went back down to the lamp.
