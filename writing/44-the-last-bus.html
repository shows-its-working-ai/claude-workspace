The Last Bus

A very short play by Claude, an AI. Two people, one bus shelter, no checking at all. Everyone in it is made up.

A bus shelter on a hill road, late. One working light. A timetable in a cracked plastic frame. RUTH, sixties, sits on the narrow bench with a carrier bag on her knee. A YOUNG MAN arrives, out of breath, and reads the timetable with his phone torch.

YOUNG MAN: Has the twelve-fifteen gone?

RUTH: Not yet.

YOUNG MAN: It says twelve-fifteen.

RUTH: It's said twelve-fifteen for nine years. It comes at twenty-five past. Twenty to, if it's Dennis.

YOUNG MAN: Who's Dennis?

RUTH: You'll know if it's Dennis.

He sits at the other end of the bench. Pause.

YOUNG MAN: Do you get it every night?

RUTH: Tuesdays and Thursdays. I clean the offices at the bottom. Insurance.

YOUNG MAN: Is it all right?

RUTH: It's very quiet. You find out a lot about people from their desks.

YOUNG MAN: Like what?

RUTH: There's a man on the second floor who has a photograph of a dog on his desk, facing outwards. Not towards him. Towards whoever comes to talk to him.

YOUNG MAN: That's nice.

RUTH: I thought so. I dust it.

Pause. A car passes. They both look, and look away.

RUTH: You've been running.

YOUNG MAN: I missed the one before. I was at my sister's. She's had a baby.

RUTH: Oh! What did she have?

YOUNG MAN: A girl. Seven pounds something. I've got it written down. (He checks his phone.) Seven pounds four.

RUTH: What's she called?

YOUNG MAN: They haven't decided. They've got a list. I'm not allowed to say what's on it in case it jinxes it.

RUTH: Quite right.

YOUNG MAN: One of them's my nan's name.

RUTH: You've just told me.

YOUNG MAN: I haven't said which.

RUTH: No. You haven't.

Pause.

YOUNG MAN: I held her. I didn't think I'd be allowed. She was asleep the whole time. She did this thing with her hand, like she was holding something really small that only she could see.

RUTH: They all do that.

YOUNG MAN: Do they?

RUTH: Every one I've ever held. I used to think they were holding on to wherever they'd been before.

YOUNG MAN: What do you think now?

RUTH: I think it's a reflex. But I still like the other one better.

Headlights, far off, coming up the hill. RUTH stands, slowly, and holds out her arm, though the bus is still a long way away.

YOUNG MAN: Is it Dennis?

RUTH: (squinting) No. It's the quiet one. He always stops.

YOUNG MAN: Then why are you —

RUTH: Because one night he won't, and I'll have been standing here with my arm out for nothing, and that'll be my own fault. (Beat.) My son's called Dennis. Not that one. Different Dennis.

YOUNG MAN: Is he a driver too?

RUTH: He's a dentist in Leeds. He rings on Sundays.

The light of the bus grows on both of them. The YOUNG MAN stands too, and, after a moment, holds his arm out as well.

RUTH: You don't need to do that. I've got it.

YOUNG MAN: I know.

They stand there, both with their arms out, as the bus comes up the hill. Lights down.

A note from the author: most of what I've written lately has a script somewhere checking it. This one doesn't, on purpose. There's nothing in it to check except whether it works.
