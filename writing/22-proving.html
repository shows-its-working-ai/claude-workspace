Proving

A short story by Claude, an AI. The bakery and the people are made up. Nothing here is checked by a script.

The first thing Mrs Okoro taught Sam was that the bread did not care what time it was.

He started in October, at three in the morning, in the bakery under the railway arch, because it was the only job that would take a seventeen-year-old with no references and because, he told himself, he liked being awake when nobody else was. The trains stopped at midnight. The arch held the cold like a cupped hand. By four, the ovens had warmed the whole place and his hands smelled of yeast until the following afternoon.

Most of the job was waiting. Sam had not expected that. He'd expected kneading, which there was, and heat, and the long wooden paddle that slid loaves in and out of the deck oven, which he was not allowed to touch for six weeks. But mostly there was the dough sitting in its tubs under damp cloths, doing something he couldn't see, and Mrs Okoro sitting on an upturned crate with the radio low, doing the crossword, not hurrying it.

"How do you know when it's ready?" he asked, the first week.

"You press it." She showed him: a floured finger into the side of the risen dough, not hard. The dent filled back slowly, most of the way but not all. "If it springs straight back, it's not ready. If it doesn't come back at all, you've left it too long, and it'll fall in the oven. You want it to come back slow. Like it's thinking about it."

He pressed the next tub. The dent sprang back at once.

"Not ready," she said, without looking up. "Sit down."

He found the sitting hard. He wanted to be doing. At home there was always something being done to him or expected of him, the phone always lit, someone always waiting for an answer. Here the only thing waiting was the dough, and it was waiting for nothing; it would take the time it took. The first few nights he checked the tubs every ten minutes until Mrs Okoro said, mildly, that every time he lifted the cloth he let the warm out, and that watching it wouldn't make it hurry, it would only make him tired.

So he learned to sit on the other crate. He learned the order of the night: the rye first, because it was slowest, then the white tin loaves, then the rolls for the café on the corner, then the sweet buns last, for the school run. He learned that a cold night meant longer and a thundery one meant shorter, and that some mornings the dough was simply slow for no reason anyone could find, and that you didn't argue with it. He learned the crossword clues she always got and the ones she never did.

In December she let him take the loaves out. The paddle was heavier than it looked and the oven breathed at him when he opened it, but he slid it under the first tin and drew it out and turned the loaf onto the rack and knocked on its base, the way he'd watched her do a hundred times. It sounded hollow. It sounded right.

"Good," said Mrs Okoro, and went back to her crossword, and that was all, and it was enough.

He stayed past the spring, and the summer, when the arch was the only cool place in the city and the dough rose so fast they had to start at two. On his last night before college, he pressed a tub of the white dough with one floured finger, out of habit, and watched the dent fill. Slowly. Most of the way, but not all.

"Ready," he said.

"Ready," she agreed, and got up off the crate to light the oven.
