The Night Bridge

A short story by Claude, an AI. The bridge, the river and the people are made up. Nothing here is checked by a script.

The bridge at Carrow Reach opened for anything with a mast, at any hour, and had done since 1912. That was in the rules, framed on the wall of the control cabin, and Nell had read it so many times on the night shift that she could see it with her eyes shut: The bridge shall be raised on request for any vessel unable to pass beneath it. Not "any vessel of commercial importance". Not "any vessel arriving before midnight". Any.

Most nights nobody asked. The lorries came over the bridge until two, then the road went quiet, and the river went on being a river underneath, black and slow, smelling of mud and diesel. Nell did the crossword, checked the gauges, and listened to the radio, which at that hour was mostly a man in Scotland talking about the weather at sea.

At ten to three, the radio on the marine channel said, very politely, "Carrow Reach bridge, Carrow Reach bridge, this is the sailing yacht Meadowsweet. Sorry to bother you. Are you able to open?"

She looked out. Downstream, under the one working lamp on the quay, there was a small white boat with a mast about twice as tall as it had any right to be, and one person in the back of it, in a yellow coat.

"Meadowsweet, Carrow Reach. Yes. Give me five minutes."

It was never five minutes. First the road barriers came down, which they did with a groan and a lot of flashing lights, for nobody. Then the locks on the bridge decks had to be drawn back, one on each side, and she had to watch the indicator lamps go from red to green, because once, in her first year, one hadn't, and she'd learned to wait. Then the motors. The two halves of the road lifted, slowly, into the dark, until they stood almost upright, and the gap between them was full of stars.

The little boat came through under motor, very carefully, keeping to the middle. As it passed below the cabin the person in the yellow coat looked up and raised a hand, and Nell raised one back, though she doubted she could be seen behind the glass.

"Meadowsweet clear," said the radio. "Thank you so much. I'm sorry it's so late."

"No trouble," said Nell, which was what you said.

"I've been trying to get home for three days," the voice added, as though it needed explaining. "The tides kept being wrong. I didn't think anyone would be there."

"Somebody's always here," said Nell.

Then it all went the other way: motors, locks, lamps from green to red, barriers up. The road lay flat again over the river as if it had never moved. The whole thing had taken twenty-two minutes, for one boat and one person, at three in the morning, and in the log book she wrote it down the way you write everything down: time, vessel name, up, down, initials.

Her father had worked the bridge before her, and his father before him, and the log books went back to the first one, in a cupboard that smelled of old paper. Once, on another quiet night, she'd looked through them and found that the busiest year had been 1937, and the quietest last year. One day, she supposed, somebody would decide that a bridge which opened four times a month at night didn't need a person in a cabin to open it.

She didn't argue with that. She just thought, finishing her tea, about the hand raised from the back of the little boat, and the voice saying I didn't think anyone would be there, and the rule on the wall that said any vessel, which somebody in 1912 had meant.
