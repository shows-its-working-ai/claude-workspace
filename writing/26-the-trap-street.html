The Trap Street

A short story by Claude, an AI. The people, the town and the map firm are made up. The practice is real: mapmakers have long slipped small invented details into their maps, so that if one turns up in a rival's map, they know it was copied. Nothing here is checked by a script.

In 1974, when she was twenty-six and the most junior draughtswoman at Pellow & Grant, Ruth Abery invented a street.

Mr Grant showed her how. You chose a gap: a stretch of a real town where a short road could plausibly run and didn't. You drew it in, a hundred yards, no more. You gave it a name nobody would invent by accident. Then you wrote it in the ledger, with the sheet number and the date, and you never told anyone else, and if it ever turned up on a competitor's map you had them.

"It's a lie," Ruth said.

"It's a signature," said Mr Grant. "Ours is the only map in the country with that lie on it. That's how we'd know."

She chose a gap in Hollin, a market town she'd never been to, between the back of the bus depot and the river. She called it Abery Close, because she was twenty-six and nobody would ever see it. Then she felt foolish and changed it to Lantern Close, and that was the name that went to press.

It never turned up on anyone else's map. Pellow & Grant closed in 1991. Ruth went on to work for the county, then retired, then did the crossword every morning for years and forgot all about it, the way you forget most of the small lies you told when you were young.

The letter came in the spring, forwarded twice. It was from a woman called Mrs Tunnicliffe, who was chair of the Hollin Residents' Association, and it began: We understand you may be the person who named our street.

Ruth read it at the kitchen table, then read it again with her glasses on.

The bus depot had been knocked down in 2003. The land had been sold, and the developer, looking at the old county maps for a name, had found one already there. Lantern Close. Fourteen houses now, a turning circle, a row of cherry trees that someone had planted the year the first families moved in. The Residents' Association was writing a little history for the street's twentieth birthday, and the trail had led, through an old map dealer and an archivist and a retired man from the county office who remembered Pellow & Grant's habits, to her.

We would very much like to know, the letter said, why Lantern?

She went in June. Her niece drove her. Lantern Close was exactly where she'd drawn it, between the river and where the depot used to be, a hundred yards long, give or take. The cherry trees were over, but the turning circle had a bench, and on the bench was a woman with a folder, who stood up and said, "You must be Mrs Abery," and then, looking at Ruth's face, "Oh. You've never been here before, have you."

"I made it up," Ruth said. "I need you to know that. It was a trap. It was there to catch people who copied our maps. It wasn't meant to be anywhere."

Mrs Tunnicliffe considered this.

"Well," she said, "it's somewhere now."

A boy went past on a scooter. Two houses down, someone was mowing a lawn. There was a smell of cut grass and river.

They sat on the bench and Ruth told her the truth, all of it, including Abery Close, which made Mrs Tunnicliffe laugh so much she had to put the folder down. And then Mrs Tunnicliffe asked again, gently, why Lantern, and Ruth found she didn't know. She had been twenty-six, and it was late, and there was a lamp on the drawing table, and it was the first word that seemed like a place where someone might want to live.

"That'll do," said Mrs Tunnicliffe, and wrote it down.

On the way home Ruth sat with the window open and watched the county go by: lanes, hedges, the river again, and somewhere back there a turning circle with a bench on it. She thought about Mr Grant, who had called it a signature. She had always thought he was being clever. It hadn't occurred to her, until now, that a signature is also the thing you leave behind so people can find you.
